# Rosetta and Philae

Rosetta was a mission of the European Space Agency (ESA), with contributions from member
states and NASA, sent to orbit and study a comet in unprecedented detail. It launched on March
2, 2004, from the Guiana Space Centre in Kourou, French Guiana, aboard an Ariane 5 rocket, and
carried a small lander named Philae, built by a consortium led by the German Aerospace Center
(DLR).

## A ten-year cruise

To reach its target, comet 67P/Churyumov-Gerasimenko, Rosetta needed ten years and a series of
gravity assists at Earth and Mars, plus a long hibernation period to conserve power during the
coldest, most distant part of its path. This patient approach differed from the faster, direct
flybys used by earlier comet missions and let Rosetta match the comet's speed rather than
racing past it.

## Orbiting a comet

Rosetta arrived at 67P in August 2014 and became the first spacecraft to orbit a comet, mapping
its oddly shaped, two-lobed nucleus, often described as looking like a rubber duck. Instruments
aboard the orbiter, including a mass spectrometer called ROSINA, detected organic molecules and
measured the ratio of heavy to light water in the comet's vapor, a clue relevant to whether
comets like 67P delivered water to early Earth.

## Philae's landing

On November 12, 2014, Rosetta released Philae, which made the first-ever soft landing on a
comet nucleus. The landing did not go entirely to plan: Philae's anchoring harpoons failed to
fire, and the lander bounced before settling in a shadowed spot where its solar panels received
too little light. Philae still managed to transmit data for about sixty hours before its
batteries ran down, and it was briefly heard from again the following year as the comet moved
closer to the Sun.

## Legacy

Rosetta continued studying 67P as it swung around the Sun, watching jets of gas and dust
intensify, before mission controllers ended the mission on September 30, 2016, by gently
guiding the orbiter down onto the comet's surface. The techniques Rosetta pioneered for
rendezvous with a small, weakly gravitating body later informed the approach strategies used by
Hayabusa2 and OSIRIS-REx at their respective asteroids.
