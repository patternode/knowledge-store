Hayabusa2

Hayabusa2 is a sample-return mission operated by the Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency (JAXA), a follow-up to the original Hayabusa mission that had returned a small amount of asteroid dust in 2010 despite serious technical troubles along the way. Hayabusa2 was built to be more robust and to bring back a larger, better-documented sample.

Launch and cruise

The spacecraft launched on December 3, 2014, from the Tanegashima Space Center in Japan aboard an H-IIA rocket. It reached its target, the near-Earth asteroid Ryugu, in June 2018 after an Earth gravity assist along the way.

At Ryugu

Ryugu turned out to be a dark, spinning-top-shaped rubble pile. Hayabusa2 released small hopping rovers and a lander called MASCOT, developed with the German Aerospace Center (DLR) and the French space agency CNES, to study the surface up close. In a striking demonstration, Hayabusa2 fired a small copper projectile, called the Small Carry-on Impactor, into Ryugu to blast out an artificial crater, then descended to collect a sample of the freshly exposed subsurface material, a technique no earlier mission had attempted.

Sample return capsule

Hayabusa2 touched down on Ryugu twice to collect surface and subsurface samples, storing them in a sealed capsule. On December 5, 2020, the spacecraft released this capsule, which parachuted into the Australian outback near Woomera and was recovered within hours. Laboratory analysis of the roughly five grams of returned material found amino acids and other organic compounds, adding to evidence that asteroids like Ryugu could have delivered such building blocks to the early Earth.

Extended mission

After dropping off its sample capsule, Hayabusa2 did not stop; the spacecraft, still carrying fuel and working instruments, was retargeted for a flyby of a much smaller asteroid, 1998 KY26, expected in the 2030s, extending a mission that began as a follow-up to Hayabusa's earlier, troubled flight.
