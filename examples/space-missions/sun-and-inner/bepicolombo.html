BepiColombo

BepiColombo is a joint mission of the European Space Agency (ESA) and the Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency (JAXA) to study Mercury, the innermost planet, which has been visited by spacecraft only twice before: NASA's Mariner 10 flybys in the 1970s and the MESSENGER orbiter in the following decade. The mission is named after Giuseppe (Bepi) Colombo, an Italian scientist who worked out the gravity-assist trajectories that made the Mariner 10 mission possible.

Two orbiters in one

BepiColombo actually carries two separate science orbiters stacked together for the journey: ESA's Mercury Planetary Orbiter (MPO) and JAXA's Mercury Magnetospheric Orbiter (MMO, also called Mio), joined to a transfer module and a solar-electric propulsion stage for the cruise. The two orbiters will separate and operate independently only once they reach Mercury orbit.

Launch and long cruise

BepiColombo launched on October 20, 2018, from the Guiana Space Centre in Kourou aboard an Ariane 5 rocket, the same launch vehicle family that had carried Rosetta into space. Reaching Mercury is unusually difficult because the Sun's gravity keeps pulling a spacecraft inward, so BepiColombo relies on a long sequence of gravity assists, one at Earth, two at Venus, and six at Mercury itself, combined with its ion thrusters, to gradually slow down enough to be captured into orbit in late 2026.

Science goals

Once in orbit, the two BepiColombo spacecraft will study Mercury's unusually dense iron core, its thin exosphere, its surprisingly strong magnetic field for such a small planet, and permanently shadowed polar craters where earlier missions found signs of water ice. During its long cruise, BepiColombo has already used its instruments to make science observations during its Mercury flybys, ahead of full orbital operations.
