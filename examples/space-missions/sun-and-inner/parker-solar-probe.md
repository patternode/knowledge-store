# Parker Solar Probe

Parker Solar Probe is a NASA mission managed by the Johns Hopkins University Applied Physics
Laboratory, built to fly closer to the Sun than any spacecraft before it. It launched on August
12, 2018, from Cape Canaveral aboard a Delta IV Heavy rocket, one of the most powerful launch
vehicles available at the time, needed to shed enough of Earth's orbital motion for the probe
to fall so deep into the Sun's gravity well.

## Naming

The mission is named after Eugene Parker, the astrophysicist who in 1958 first proposed the
existence of the solar wind, a continuous outward stream of charged particles from the Sun.
Parker Solar Probe is the first NASA spacecraft named for a living person at the time of
naming, and Parker attended the launch.

## Getting close to the Sun

Rather than approach the Sun directly, the probe uses repeated gravity assists at Venus,
gradually tightening its orbit over several years. Each Venus flyby trades a little of the
probe's energy to bring its closest approach to the Sun, called perihelion, nearer with every
pass. By the mid-2020s these flybys had brought Parker Solar Probe to within about six million
kilometers of the Sun's surface, deep inside the corona, faster and closer than any prior
mission.

## Surviving the heat

To survive temperatures that can exceed 1,300 degrees Celsius on its sunward side, the probe
is shielded by a carbon-composite heat shield roughly the thickness of a fist, which keeps the
instruments and electronics behind it near room temperature. Its instruments include a
magnetometer, a solar wind detector, and a wide-field imager called WISPR that photographs the
corona and solar wind from the side.

## Discoveries

Parker Solar Probe has found small, ubiquitous magnetic "switchbacks" in the solar wind, zigzag
kinks in the magnetic field that may help explain how the corona is heated to temperatures far
hotter than the Sun's visible surface, a long-standing puzzle in solar physics. The mission
continues its campaign of ever-closer approaches, complementing ESA's Solar Orbiter, launched
in 2020, which studies the Sun from a different vantage point including views of its poles.
