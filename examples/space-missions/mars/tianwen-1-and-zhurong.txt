BRIEFING NOTE: TIANWEN-1 AND ZHURONG
Prepared for internal circulation, knowledge-store demo corpus

OVERVIEW
Tianwen-1 is the China National Space Administration's (CNSA) first independent interplanetary
mission, combining an orbiter, a lander, and a small rover named Zhurong in a single launch to
Mars, an ambitious approach that no other agency had attempted on a first Mars mission.
"Tianwen" translates roughly as "questions to heaven." The mission launched on July 23, 2020,
from the Wenchang Spacecraft Launch Site on Hainan Island aboard a Long March 5 rocket, one of
China's most powerful launch vehicles.

ARRIVAL AND LANDING
Tianwen-1 entered orbit around Mars in February 2021, arriving in the same general window as
Perseverance, though the two missions were unrelated. After several months surveying candidate
landing sites from orbit, the lander and rover descended to Utopia Planitia, a large plain in
Mars's northern hemisphere, touching down on May 15, 2021. This made China the second country,
after the United States, to successfully operate a rover on the Martian surface.

ZHURONG
The rover was named Zhurong after a fire god in Chinese mythology, chosen through a public
naming process. Solar-powered and roughly the size of a golf cart, Zhurong carried cameras, a
ground-penetrating radar, and instruments to study surface composition and the Martian
magnetic environment. It far outlasted its planned ninety-day mission, but entered a planned
hibernation for the Martian winter in May 2022 and did not wake as expected, leaving its status
uncertain in the following years even as the orbiter continued operating.

ORBITER
The Tianwen-1 orbiter carried its own science payload, including a high-resolution camera and a
subsurface radar, and continued mapping Mars and relaying communications after the landing,
extending the mission's science return well beyond the rover's own activities.

CONTEXT
Tianwen-1 arrived at Mars alongside the United Arab Emirates' Hope orbiter and NASA's
Perseverance rover, making early 2021 an unusually crowded period for Mars arrivals from three
different space programs within about two weeks of each other.
