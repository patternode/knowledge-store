BRIEFING NOTE: NEW HORIZONS
Prepared for internal circulation, knowledge-store demo corpus

OVERVIEW
New Horizons is a NASA mission, managed by the Johns Hopkins University Applied Physics
Laboratory (APL) with Alan Stern of the Southwest Research Institute as principal
investigator, sent to make the first close flyby of Pluto and later the Kuiper Belt. It
launched on January 19, 2006, from Cape Canaveral on an Atlas V 551 rocket, the same class of
vehicle later used to launch Juno.

TRAJECTORY
The spacecraft left Earth faster than any previous probe, and it still took nine and a half
years to reach Pluto. It received a gravity assist at Jupiter in February 2007, which also let
it study the giant planet's atmosphere and its moon Io in passing, echoing observations Galileo
and Voyager had made decades earlier.

PLUTO FLYBY
New Horizons flew past Pluto on July 14, 2015, at a distance of about 12,500 kilometers,
returning the first detailed images of the dwarf planet and its largest moon, Charon. The
flyby revealed a young, geologically active surface, including a heart-shaped plain of nitrogen
ice informally named Tombaugh Regio after Pluto's discoverer, Clyde Tombaugh, and mountain
ranges made of water ice. Instruments included the Long Range Reconnaissance Imager (LORRI)
and the Ralph visible and infrared imager.

ARROKOTH AND THE KUIPER BELT
On January 1, 2019, New Horizons flew past a small Kuiper Belt object nicknamed Ultima Thule
and later formally named Arrokoth, the most distant object ever explored up close at the time.
The flyby showed Arrokoth to be a gently fused pair of lobes, evidence for a slow, gentle
process of planet-building in the outer solar system rather than violent collisions.

STATUS
New Horizons remains active and continues to return data from the outer solar system, with its
team studying possible future targets as it heads toward interstellar space, following a path
distinct from either Voyager probe.
