Cassini-Huygens

Cassini-Huygens was a joint mission by the National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA), the European Space Agency (ESA), and the Italian Space Agency (ASI) to study Saturn, its rings, and its moons. The Cassini orbiter was built and operated by the Jet Propulsion Laboratory, while the Huygens lander that it carried was built by ESA.

Launch and journey

The spacecraft launched on October 15, 1997, from Cape Canaveral Space Force Station on a Titan IVB rocket. Rather than flying directly to Saturn, Cassini used a series of gravity assists at Venus, Earth, and Jupiter to build up enough speed for the seven-year cruise, finally entering orbit around Saturn on July 1, 2004.

Huygens at Titan

On January 14, 2005, the Huygens probe separated from Cassini and descended by parachute through the thick atmosphere of Titan, Saturn's largest moon, landing on its surface and returning the first images and direct atmospheric measurements ever taken there. Huygens found evidence of methane rain and river-like channels carved into what appeared to be a solid, icy surface.

Discoveries at Saturn

Cassini spent thirteen years orbiting Saturn, discovering geysers of water vapor erupting from the south pole of the moon Enceladus, evidence consistent with a subsurface ocean, and returning detailed images of the planet's rings and complex weather. Instruments included the Imaging Science Subsystem (ISS), the Cassini Plasma Spectrometer (CAPS), and the Radar mapper used to chart Titan's lakes of liquid methane through the haze.

The Grand Finale

To avoid ever contaminating Enceladus or Titan with Earth microbes, Cassini ended its mission with a deliberate plunge into Saturn's atmosphere on September 15, 2017, a sequence of close ring-grazing orbits that mission engineers nicknamed the Grand Finale. Cassini's long stay at Saturn is often compared to Galileo's earlier survey of Jupiter, since both missions showed how a long-duration orbiter can reveal moons and rings that a short flyby would miss.
