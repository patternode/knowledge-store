# Voyager 1

Voyager 1 is a robotic probe launched by the National Aeronautics and Space Administration
(NASA) on September 5, 1977, from Cape Canaveral, Florida, atop a Titan IIIE rocket. It left
Earth about two weeks after its twin, Voyager 2, even though its mission number is lower,
because it was placed on a faster trajectory. Both spacecraft were built and are operated by
the Jet Propulsion Laboratory (JPL) in Pasadena, California.

## Mission profile

Voyager 1's primary targets were Jupiter and Saturn. It used gravity assists at each planet,
borrowing a small amount of orbital energy to bend its path and gain speed, a technique that
let the mission reach the outer solar system far faster than a direct trajectory would allow.
The spacecraft carries a suite of cameras and fields-and-particles instruments, including a
magnetometer and plasma science experiment, though its cameras were powered down after the
Saturn encounter to save energy for the long cruise ahead.

## Key results

At Jupiter in 1979, Voyager 1 returned close-up images of the Great Red Spot and discovered
volcanic activity on the moon Io, the first active volcanoes seen anywhere beyond Earth. At
Saturn in 1980, it captured detailed views of the ring system and the hazy moon Titan. After
Saturn, Voyager 1 was directed on a trajectory out of the plane of the planets, a path that
Voyager 2 did not follow, so the two probes explored the outer heliosphere from different
directions.

## Interstellar space and the Golden Record

In August 2012, Voyager 1 crossed the heliopause and became the first human-made object
confirmed to reach interstellar space, where the instruments detected a marked drop in
particles from the Sun and a rise in galactic cosmic rays. Both Voyager spacecraft carry a
Golden Record, a gold-plated phonograph disc bearing sounds, images, and music intended as a
message should either probe ever be found. As of the mid-2020s, Voyager 1 remains in
intermittent contact with the Deep Space Network, its plutonium-fueled generators producing
less power every year, and mission engineers periodically retire instruments to conserve the
remaining electricity.
