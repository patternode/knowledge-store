# Galileo

The Galileo mission was NASA's first spacecraft to orbit an outer planet. It launched on
October 18, 1989, carried to space aboard the Space Shuttle Atlantis and released with an
Inertial Upper Stage booster that sent it toward Jupiter. Rather than fly there directly,
Galileo used gravity assists at Venus and twice at Earth, a roundabout path engineers called
the VEEGA trajectory (Venus-Earth-Earth Gravity Assist), before arriving at Jupiter in
December 1995.

## Atmospheric probe

As it approached Jupiter, Galileo released a small atmospheric probe that plunged into the
planet's clouds, transmitting data for about 58 minutes before pressure and heat destroyed it.
The probe measured wind speeds, composition, and lightning activity far below the visible
cloud tops, findings that surprised scientists who had expected more water vapor than the
probe actually detected.

## Orbiter and moons

The main Galileo orbiter spent nearly eight years touring the Jovian system, well beyond its
planned two-year mission, making repeated close passes of Jupiter's four largest moons: Io,
Europa, Ganymede, and Callisto (the same moons Galileo Galilei discovered in 1610, for whom the
mission was named). Its instruments, including a Solid-State Imaging camera and a magnetometer,
found strong evidence that Europa harbors a liquid water ocean beneath its icy crust, a result
that later shaped the case for the Europa Clipper mission. Galileo also detected a magnetic
field generated by Ganymede, the only moon in the solar system known to have one, and observed
plumes and lava flows on the volcanically active Io.

## A stuck antenna and a fiery end

Galileo's main high-gain antenna failed to fully unfold after launch, forcing engineers to
relay most of its data through a much slower backup antenna, a setback the mission team
compensated for with onboard data compression and patience. Galileo also happened to be well
placed to observe fragments of Comet Shoemaker-Levy 9 striking Jupiter in 1994. With its fuel
nearly spent, controllers deliberately steered Galileo into Jupiter's atmosphere on September
21, 2003, to prevent any risk of the spacecraft later crashing into and contaminating Europa,
a precaution that Cassini would later echo at Saturn.
